MedicalQA: Caregiver Q&A from Dementia-Care Videos

AIM Lab, Santa Clara University · ongoing

LLMsvLLMLLM-as-judgeBERTopicWhisperSupabase

Caregivers of people with dementia don't have time to watch hours of training videos. We're building MedicalQA to turn those videos into questions and answers they can search and ask about. It extends our lab's MentorQA framework.

I brought in the Teepa Snow dementia-care videos and grew the dataset with Whisper transcription. I added seeding, temperature and decoding controls so we compare pipelines fairly, and labeled the topics with BERTopic and a group of 9 LLMs.

For evaluation, I built a panel of LLM judges (vLLM on the university's GPU cluster plus hosted APIs) and checked it against human raters with Fleiss' kappa, Gwet's AC1 and Krippendorff's alpha. I also co-built the annotation site (JavaScript and Supabase) where people rate the generated pairs.

McMiner-in-the-Loop: Misconception-Aware Code Tutoring

Graduate research with the AIM Lab, Santa Clara University · Catherine Awad, Ruiwen Guan, Hanieh Hedayati (Advisor: Dr. Oana Ignat)

LLMsPyTorchLoRADPOGRPOpass@kEvoCodeBenchDevEval

Two existing systems each do half the job. TRAVER is a tutor that talks to students but can't tell what they misunderstand. McMiner finds misconceptions from code but doesn't tutor. We connected them: every round, the student's misconception goes into the tutor's next prompt so the hint can target it. We tested mined, class-level and true (oracle) misconceptions with two tutors on EvoCodeBench and DevEval.

It didn't help. Using pass@k, paired sign tests and bootstrap confidence intervals, none of the versions beat the tutor with no misconceptions, even when we gave it the true ones. When we checked the miner's reports during tutoring, most of them were wrong.

On the training side, our LoRA reward models and a DPO tutor stayed close to chance at ranking tutor replies. I also implemented GRPO with a sandboxed unit test reward and raised Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B's MBPP pass@1 from 0.50 to 0.57 (p < 0.001), with KL, dynamic sampling and reward poisoning ablations.

To run the long multi-round evaluations, we built a GPU harness that pauses and resumes when the server goes down, and a web app that replays baseline and McMiner dialogues side by side, with a live mode that runs new sessions against real LLM APIs.

Can a Phone Replace the GPU? On-Device 3D Pose for Movement Coaching

Santa Clara University · ongoing (interim results, Nov 2026)

3D Human Pose EstimationModel DistillationEdge ML / ONNXBenchmarkingSAM 3D BodySlurm HPCPython

Simon compares a follower's movement to a trainer's 3D recording. Right now every take runs SAM 3D Body on a cloud GPU, which costs money and means uploading the video. I'm testing if a model small enough for a phone can handle the follower's side, and how to tell if it's good enough. Joint error isn't the real test. The real test is whether the person still gets the same feedback.

I built the benchmark: 27 clips across nine movement types, with SAM 3D Body reference poses generated on the university's GPU cluster. I ran phone-sized models against it: InstantHMR (a recent phone mesh model by other authors) and Google's BlazePose GHUM in three sizes. Every output goes through Simon's own fault analysis, so I compare the actual coaching (which faults get flagged, the score, the grade) and not just joint positions. I've also started building a training set from licence-cleared data (Harmony4D and permissively licensed COCO images) labeled with SAM 3D Body.

So far, InstantHMR passed Simon's joint accuracy bar on 24 of 27 clips (median 5.5 cm from SAM 3D Body, 22 ms a frame on a laptop CPU). But it gave the exact same faults on only 1 of 18 follower takes (fault F1 around 0.50), and it struggled most with floor and seated poses. BlazePose passed the joint bar on 4 of 27 clips at most. The takeaway: passing the usual joint accuracy bar doesn't mean the feedback stays the same, so feedback agreement should be the measure. Next I'll fine-tune the phone model and judge it on that.

Medical Anomaly Detection with Vision-Language Models

Course project · team of four

CLIPMVFAPyTorchFew-shot learning

Labeled medical images are hard to get, so we wanted to see how well a general vision-language model can find anomalies in chest X-rays with only a few labeled examples. We built on MVFA (Multi-level Visual Feature Adaptation) on top of CLIP (ViT-L/14). Images and medical text prompts go into the same space, and the anomaly score comes from comparing an image to descriptions of normal and abnormal findings. We used the RSNA pneumonia dataset (about 29k chest X-rays, filtered to 17k) and measured AUROC from zero-shot up to 16 shots.

We tried sixteen setups, comparing scoring heads (including a small MLP that scores straight from image features), adapter regularization, medical prompt ensembles, and combining cosine similarity, Mahalanobis distance and energy scores. I worked on the scoring side: the scoring methods, the score fusion and the regularization.

Everything ran on V100 GPUs on the university's WAVE cluster, so there's no public repo. The full results are in the report below.

Hydration Automation

EPIC Lab, Santa Clara University · Farm IoT platform

FlutterNode.js/ExpressArduino C++LoRaNFCGoogle MapsPostHog

An IoT platform for monitoring agricultural hydration systems in real time, built at Santa Clara University's EPIC Lab.

App: I built the Flutter app: live sensor dashboards with a farm health score, magic link sign-in, and translations with dark mode. Farmers set up a farm by putting it on a map and drawing the field's edge, either by tapping or by walking it with the phone's GPS. They add devices by scanning the printed labels (QR, with NFC to identify a unit), and can rename stations and sensors, move them between farms, release them, or save their install location from the phone's GPS. Sign-up is invite only, with a "Get a device" page instead.

Firmware & backend: I built the SAMD21/LoRa firmware in Arduino C++: uploads that wait for an acknowledgement and get batched, an offline queue, NTP-synced clocks, device whitelists refreshed over the air, battery telemetry, and rejecting data from units that aren't on the list. I co-developed the Node.js/Express backend: claiming devices with hashed label tokens that rotate, ownership checks, farm boundaries, batched IoT uploads, a simulated test fleet, and PostHog feature flags.

I also built the public website (two languages, light and dark themes) and set up the CI pipeline, commit hooks, design system checks and integration docs the team works from. Everything goes through reviewed pull requests.

GPU Cluster Analysis: Shorter Queues and Root Causes

MantisGrid AI Hackathon 2026 · Best Solution, Track 2

Scheduler simulationSlurmRoot-cause analysisPythonpandasDocker

We were given a $1.49M GPU cluster bill. Most teams focused on cutting the cost. We asked something else: why are researchers stuck in the queue when the cluster is mostly idle, and what's really causing the failures behind the bill?

The queue was a quota problem, not a hardware problem. I built a scheduler simulator and replayed all 74,838 jobs that could start. In 90% of waits longer than an hour, the researcher had already hit their own limit on running jobs, while a median of 287 out of 450 GPUs were free. Letting people go past their limit while the cluster is under 70% used, plus ending allocations that sit idle for an hour, cut the modeled waiting from 1,326 to 59 person-hours. That's 96% less, with no new hardware. Reordering the queue didn't help, because the scheduler was already close to first come, first served, with backfill working.

A safer option using prediction. Letting people go past their limit only reacts after the fact, so we also tried a credits pool: when a researcher is away, they lend their unused quota for a limited time. Based on the limits people requested, it did nothing, because they asked for about 2,000 times more than they used. Based on each person's predicted runtime (their past p90), it cut waiting by 11%, or 21% with the idle timeout, and lenders were only blocked for 4.3 researcher-hours over 18 weeks. Predicting each person's quota from their recent demand didn't work. Demand was too bursty and waiting went up.

Root causes, not symptoms. The API said to take down the five machines with the most findings. When we traced those findings through its causal graph, the problem was one broken Slurm array script, not five broken machines. A 121-machine "incident" came down to one storage volume. The one machine that was actually broken ranked 21st by number of findings. We found it by checking which crashes followed the machine and not the user (SIGBUS, three unrelated users), and it could have been caught six days earlier.

Then the cost: about $359K could be saved ($202K to $491K), mostly from GPUs that were allocated but never used. Taking those back first, before cutting capacity, keeps the queues from getting worse. I built the analysis pipeline, the scheduler simulator and the CFO dashboard, where a slider steps through precomputed replays of the quota options.
